The 50/30/20 Rule for a Single Income Household

The 50/30/20 rule splits take-home pay into about 50% needs, 30% wants, and 20% savings. It was popularized by Elizabeth Warren’s book All Your Worth as a simple way to check whether a budget is balanced. On one income it works best as a comparison, not a pass/fail test.

What goes in each bucket

Needs (50%) are the costs of keeping the household running: housing, utilities, groceries, transport, insurance, childcare, and minimum debt payments. Wants (30%) are the flexible parts: eating out, streaming, hobbies, fun money, holidays. Savings (20%) covers the emergency fund, retirement saving, and extra debt payments beyond the minimum. In this planner, sinking funds count toward savings, because the money is set aside for a future cost rather than spent now.

Why 50% needs is hard on one income

Housing and childcare do not shrink just because a household has one earner. In many cities rent alone takes 40% or more of a single paycheck, and childcare can take another large share. If your needs land at 60% or even 65%, that is a description of your costs, not a failure of discipline. The useful question is not “why am I not at 50%?” but “which need is the biggest, and is there a cheaper way to meet it?” Usually the answer is one line — housing, childcare, or a car payment — and the rule has done its job by pointing at it.

The 60/20/20 split for high fixed costs

When needs genuinely run high, a 60/20/20 split is the honest version of the same idea: about 60% needs, 20% wants, 20% savings. The savings share stays at 20% because that is the part that builds the buffer. What shrinks is wants, not savings. Cutting wants to 20% hurts less than it sounds, because wants are the only bucket you control week to week — and a smaller wants budget with a clear number is easier to keep than a vague promise to “spend less.”

The austerity split for lean months

Some months are simply tight: a car repair, a gap between jobs, a medical bill. For a short stretch, a 70/15/15 split — 70% needs, 15% wants, 15% savings — keeps the essentials covered while still moving something into savings. Treat it as a season, not a permanent plan. If every month is an austerity month, the problem is income or a fixed cost, and the budget should be showing that clearly enough to act on.

How to use the split guide in the planner

Pick a guide from the dropdown and the planner compares your actual buckets with it, line by line: “Your needs are 62% vs the 50% guide.” The comparison is the point — it tells you where the money is going relative to a simple benchmark. You can also set a custom split if none of the three fits. If your custom numbers do not add to 100, the planner will say so.

What the rule cannot tell you

50/30/20 says nothing about which needs are too expensive, how much emergency fund you need, or what to do when income drops. It is a one-line check, not a plan. Use it once a month to see the shape of your budget, then do the real work in the categories: the due days, the sinking funds, and the actual-versus-planned check.
