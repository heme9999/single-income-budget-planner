How to Budget on One Income: A Step-by-Step Plan

Budgeting on one income is not a different kind of budgeting. It is the same method with less slack, so the order matters more: needs first, then savings, then wants — and every dollar assigned before the month starts.

Step 1: Find your real monthly take-home pay

Use the amount that actually lands in your account, after tax and payroll deductions — not your salary figure. If you are paid weekly, multiply one paycheck by 52 and divide by 12. If you are paid every two weeks, multiply by 26 and divide by 12. The planner’s pay-frequency selector does this conversion for you. If your pay changes month to month, use the average of the last three months; the irregular income guide covers that case in detail.

Step 2: List the needs first

Write down every bill you must pay to keep the household running: housing, utilities, food, transport, insurance, minimum debt payments, and childcare if it applies. Put the due day next to each one. When everything is listed, add them up. This total is the floor your income has to clear. If the floor is higher than your take-home pay, you have found the problem early — before the month starts — and that is the point of the exercise.

Step 3: Add savings and sinking funds

Before wants, add two lines. First, an emergency-fund transfer, even a small one — a fixed amount on payday beats a large amount you never get around to. Second, sinking funds: annual or twice-yearly costs divided by 12. Car insurance, school costs, holidays, car repairs. A $1,200 yearly bill is $100 a month, and treating it as a monthly bill is what stops it from becoming a crisis.

Step 4: Give the rest a job, down to $0

Whatever is left after needs and savings is your wants money — food beyond groceries, fun, streaming, eating out. Assign it too. The planner’s Left to assign number should reach $0. That does not mean your bank balance is $0; it means every dollar has a purpose before you spend it. Unassigned money is the money that quietly disappears.

Step 5: Track actual spending during the month

A plan you never check is a wish. Once or twice a week, open the planner and fill in the actual column for what you have spent. Tick the paid box when a bill is done. If a category runs over, the planner shows it immediately — move money from another category the same day, instead of discovering the gap at the end of the month.

Step 6: Close the month and copy the plan forward

At the end of the month, look at three numbers: total planned, total actual, and Left to assign. Where did actual beat planned? That is the category to adjust next month, not the category to feel bad about. Use the “Copy plan to next month” button, then change only the lines that need changing. A budget gets easier the second and third month, because most lines stay the same.

What if the numbers do not fit?

If needs alone exceed take-home pay, the honest answer is that no rearrangement of the same numbers will fix it — something structural has to change: a cheaper housing situation, a payment plan with a creditor, help with childcare costs, or more income. In that case the budget is still useful, because it shows exactly how big the gap is and which bill is driving it. That is the number to take to a landlord, a lender, or a nonprofit credit counselor. Guessing at the gap helps no one.
